# 11. Behavioral & Cross-Functional Questions

Assessing collaboration, judgment, and communication — critical for a role sitting at the intersection of clinical medicine, statistics, machine learning, and software engineering, where the consequences of an overstated or poorly-validated finding can directly affect patient care.

---

### 11.1 Tell me about a time you had to push back on an exciting-looking finding or model result — your own or a colleague's — because you weren't confident the underlying causal or statistical reasoning actually supported it. How did you handle it?

**What interviewers look for:** Given how central the confounding and causal inference rigor discussed throughout category 2 is to this field, this is one of the most important behavioral traits for the role — evidence the candidate can resist the pull toward an exciting, publication- or deployment-ready conclusion when the underlying evidence doesn't yet support it, while still communicating constructively.

---

### 11.2 Describe a time you identified a methodological flaw (confounding, leakage, a biased outcome definition) in your own or a colleague's analysis before it caused a problem. What did you do?

**What interviewers look for:** Proactive rigor and willingness to catch and raise issues, echoing the case-study review scenarios discussed throughout categories 2, 3, and 10 — strong answers describe a specific, concrete situation and how the issue was identified and resolved, not just an abstract commitment to "being careful."

---

### 11.3 Tell me about a time you had to work closely with clinicians or other domain experts who didn't share your statistical or computational background. What made that collaboration effective or difficult?

**What interviewers look for:** Given how genuinely interdisciplinary this role is, real experience and skill translating between statistical/computational reasoning and clinical judgment — strong answers show genuine curiosity about and respect for clinical expertise, not just tolerance of working alongside clinicians.

---

### 11.4 How do you personally stay current with a field that spans clinical medicine, biostatistics, machine learning, and regulatory science — areas that each move quickly and have different literatures and communities?

**What interviewers look for:** A genuine, sustainable strategy for continued interdisciplinary learning — strong answers describe specific habits (which journals, conferences, or communities they follow across these different areas) rather than a vague general claim of staying current.

---

### 11.5 Describe a time an analysis or model you were confident in didn't hold up under further scrutiny, external validation, or real-world deployment. How did you handle it, and what did you learn?

**What interviewers look for:** Genuine accountability and scientific humility — particularly relevant given how often rigorous evaluation (per categories 3.6, 6.12, 7.12) legitimately reveals that strong retrospective or single-site performance doesn't generalize — strong answers show the candidate can communicate this kind of outcome clearly and constructively rather than downplaying it.

---

### 11.6 Tell me about a time you had to explain a complex statistical or methodological limitation to a non-technical stakeholder (a clinician, an executive, a product manager). How did you make it accessible without oversimplifying to the point of overstatement?

**What interviewers look for:** Clear communication paired with genuine technical integrity — evidence the candidate can translate complexity (why an observational finding isn't the same as a causal one, why strong AUC doesn't guarantee clinical utility) for a non-expert audience without losing the substance that actually matters for the stakeholder's decision.

---

### 11.7 Tell me about a time you had to make a recommendation or decision with genuinely incomplete evidence — for example, evaluating a model without extensive external validation, or a finding with real, unaddressed confounding risk. How did you approach that uncertainty?

**What interviewers look for:** Structured reasoning under genuine uncertainty, a routine condition in this field — strong answers describe a deliberate process (being explicit about what's known versus unknown, applying appropriate sensitivity analysis or staged validation, clearly communicating residual uncertainty) rather than either false confidence or an inability to move forward.

---

### 11.8 How would you handle a situation where a supervisor or stakeholder wanted you to characterize a finding's causal strength or a model's readiness for deployment more confidently than you believed the evidence actually supported?

**What interviewers look for:** Directly probing the evidence-integrity themes threaded throughout this repo — strong answers describe specific, professional approaches to this tension (proposing accurate alternative framing, being willing to have a direct conversation about the evidentiary gap and its real clinical stakes) rather than either capitulating to overstatement or refusing to engage constructively.

---

### 11.9 Tell me about a time you had to coordinate work across multiple functions (clinical, statistical, engineering, regulatory) on a shared project with different priorities or timelines. How did you keep things aligned?

**What interviewers look for:** Real project coordination experience relevant to this field's genuinely multi-disciplinary workflow — strong answers describe specific communication and coordination practices rather than a generic claim of being "a good communicator."

---

### 11.10 Describe a time you had to advocate for additional validation, a more rigorous study design, or a more cautious rollout despite pressure (timeline, stakeholder enthusiasm) to move forward more quickly. What was the outcome?

**What interviewers look for:** Willingness to hold a rigorous line even under real pressure, particularly important given the direct patient-care stakes in this field — strong answers describe a specific, concrete situation and how the tension was navigated, ideally including how they made a persuasive, evidence-based case rather than simply asserting caution was necessary.

---

### 11.11 What draws you specifically to biomedical data science as opposed to a more general data science or machine learning role? How do you think about the genuine tension between this field's potential to improve care and the real, sometimes uncomfortable limitations of working with messy, non-randomized, real-world clinical data?

**What interviewers look for:** Authentic motivation paired with realistic, clear-eyed awareness of the field's genuine methodological challenges (echoing the honest evaluation of causal inference limitations, data quality issues, and evidence tiers threaded throughout this repo) — candidates expressing only uncritical enthusiasm tend to be a weaker fit than those who can articulate genuine excitement alongside real appreciation for why this field's specific rigor requirements exist.

---

### 11.12 Tell me about a time you received critical feedback on your work — a flawed causal claim, a fairness gap, a validation shortfall — that was hard to hear. How did you respond, and did it change your approach going forward?

**What interviewers look for:** Genuine capacity to receive and productively act on critical feedback without defensiveness — particularly relevant given how much this field's rigor depends on colleagues being willing and able to give and receive exactly this kind of challenging review.

---

### 11.13 Describe your approach to staying appropriately skeptical of an impressive-looking model result or observational finding, while still remaining genuinely open to real, valuable insight in the data. How do you personally calibrate that balance?

**What interviewers look for:** Directly probing the evaluation discipline modeled throughout this entire repo, particularly categories 2 and 3 — strong answers describe specific personal habits or checks (constructing a DAG before adjusting for confounders, insisting on external validation, checking for leakage systematically) rather than simply claiming general skepticism or general open-mindedness without a concrete process behind it.

---

### 11.14 Tell me about a time you had to balance moving a project forward efficiently against wanting to more thoroughly validate an underlying model, causal claim, or data source. How did you decide where to draw that line?

**What interviewers look for:** Evidence of proportionate, risk-based judgment — a strong candidate describes distinguishing between situations where deeper validation was clearly warranted given the actual clinical and decision-making stakes involved, versus situations where a reasonable, well-justified level of confidence was sufficient to proceed efficiently, rather than either always defaulting to maximal caution regardless of stakes or moving forward without appropriate scrutiny regardless of what's actually riding on the analysis.
