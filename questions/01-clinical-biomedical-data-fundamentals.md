# 1. Clinical & Biomedical Data Fundamentals

The foundational knowledge of what biomedical data actually is and where it comes from — essential grounding before any modeling or analysis can be trusted.

---

### 1.1 🟢 What are the major categories of biomedical data a data scientist in this field typically works with, and how do their fundamental characteristics differ?

The major categories include structured clinical data (lab results, vital signs, coded diagnoses and procedures, typically well-organized into defined fields), unstructured clinical text (physician notes, radiology reports, pathology reports, requiring natural language processing to extract usable information), medical imaging (radiology, pathology, and other image-based data, requiring specialized computer vision approaches), physiological signal and wearable/sensor data (continuous time-series measurements like heart rate, activity, or continuous glucose monitoring), genomic and other omics data (requiring the specialized bioinformatics approaches discussed in adjacent literature), and administrative/claims data (billing and insurance records, which reflect healthcare utilization and reimbursement rather than clinical truth directly). A biomedical data scientist needs to understand these categories' fundamentally different characteristics — different levels of structure, different noise and missingness patterns, different generation processes, and different degrees of reflecting genuine clinical reality versus administrative or workflow artifact — because the appropriate analytical approach, and the appropriate level of trust to place in a given data type, differs substantially across these categories rather than being a single, uniform "biomedical data" problem.

### 1.2 🟢 What is the difference between data collected for clinical care purposes versus data collected for research purposes, and why does this distinction matter for a biomedical data scientist working primarily with the former?

Data collected for clinical care purposes (most commonly electronic health record data) is generated as a byproduct of actual patient care delivery — documented by clinicians primarily to support diagnosis, treatment decisions, and billing, not primarily to support downstream research or analytics use. Data collected for research purposes (clinical trial data, prospective cohort study data) is generated through a purpose-built protocol specifically designed with the research question and required data quality in mind from the outset. This distinction matters significantly for a biomedical data scientist working primarily with clinical care data because it means the data's completeness, consistency, and structure reflect clinical workflow and documentation habits rather than a deliberately designed data collection protocol — clinical documentation varies considerably between providers and institutions, is frequently incomplete for research purposes even when clinically adequate, and can be influenced by billing incentives rather than pure clinical accuracy — a biomedical data scientist needs to treat clinical care data as a genuinely messier, less purpose-built resource requiring more validation and quality assessment effort than research-purpose data, rather than assuming EHR data carries the same inherent reliability as a well-designed research dataset.

### 1.3 🟡 What is the concept of a "computable phenotype," and why does identifying a patient cohort with a specific clinical condition from EHR data typically require more than a simple diagnosis code query?

A computable phenotype is a formally defined, typically multi-criteria algorithm for identifying patients who genuinely have a specific clinical condition or characteristic of interest within EHR data, generally combining multiple data elements (diagnosis codes, medication history, relevant lab values, and sometimes information extracted from clinical notes) rather than relying on any single data element in isolation, echoing the phenotype algorithm discussion found in adjacent precision medicine literature. Identifying a patient cohort typically requires more than a simple diagnosis code query because a single diagnosis code, on its own, is often neither sufficiently sensitive (missing genuinely affected patients whose condition wasn't captured through that specific code, perhaps because it was documented differently or the patient was seen at a different health system) nor sufficiently specific (incorrectly including patients who received the code for administrative or billing reasons unrelated to a confirmed, active diagnosis, or for a condition being ruled out rather than confirmed) — a rigorous biomedical data scientist should validate a computable phenotype's actual performance (typically checking its sensitivity and specificity against a manually chart-reviewed reference sample of patients) before relying on it to define a study cohort, rather than assuming a single diagnosis code query adequately and reliably captures the intended clinical population.

### 1.4 🟢 What is the difference between structured and unstructured clinical data, and what general analytical techniques does each require?

Structured clinical data is entered into predefined, standardized fields with a fixed, machine-readable format (a lab test result, a coded diagnosis, a medication order), directly usable for quantitative analysis without further processing. Unstructured clinical data consists of free-text narrative content (physician notes, radiology and pathology reports) that captures rich clinical detail and nuance but isn't directly machine-readable in its raw form, generally requiring natural language processing techniques — ranging from simpler rule-based pattern matching to more sophisticated clinical NLP models specifically trained to handle clinical documentation's characteristic abbreviations, negation patterns, and terminology — to extract usable, structured information. A biomedical data scientist needs to recognize that a substantial and often clinically important proportion of the most detailed and nuanced clinical information exists only in this unstructured form, meaning an analysis relying exclusively on structured data fields risks missing meaningful clinical signal that's only documented in free text, while an analysis relying on NLP-extracted unstructured data needs to account for the inherent additional uncertainty this extraction process introduces.

### 1.5 🟡 What is the concept of "informative missingness" in clinical data, and why is the common default assumption that missing data is missing completely at random (MCAR) frequently inappropriate for real-world clinical datasets?

Informative missingness refers to a situation where the fact that a particular data element is missing is itself informative about the underlying clinical scenario, rather than reflecting a purely random, unrelated data collection gap — for instance, a specific lab test being missing for a patient might reflect that the ordering clinician judged the patient clinically stable enough not to need it, which is itself informative about the patient's likely clinical status, rather than the missingness being unrelated to the patient's actual condition. The common default statistical assumption of data being "missing completely at random" (MCAR, meaning the probability of a value being missing is unrelated to both the observed and unobserved data) is frequently inappropriate for real-world clinical data specifically because clinical documentation and testing patterns are driven by genuine clinical decision-making, not by an unrelated, random process — a biomedical data scientist needs to actively consider whether missingness in a given dataset is more plausibly MCAR, missing at random (MAR, where missingness depends on observed but not unobserved data, conditionally addressable through appropriate statistical adjustment), or missing not at random (MNAR, where missingness depends on the unobserved value itself, which is genuinely difficult to fully correct for) before choosing an appropriate approach to handling missing data, since naively applying a method that assumes MCAR to genuinely informatively missing clinical data can introduce significant, hard-to-detect bias into downstream analysis.

### 1.6 🟢 What is ICD coding, and what are its known limitations as a reliable, ground-truth source of diagnostic information for research or analytics purposes?

ICD (International Classification of Diseases) coding is a standardized system for representing diagnoses and health conditions using structured codes, widely used throughout healthcare systems primarily to support billing and administrative reporting, and commonly used as a readily available proxy for diagnostic information in research and analytics given how difficult direct unstructured note extraction can be, echoing the ICD coding discussion found in adjacent precision medicine literature. Its known limitations include that codes are often selected primarily to support accurate billing and reimbursement rather than to comprehensively or precisely capture the full clinical picture, coding specificity and practice varies meaningfully across providers and institutions, and a code's presence in a patient's record doesn't always reliably indicate a confirmed, active diagnosis (it might reflect a condition being ruled out or considered in a differential diagnosis) — a biomedical data scientist should treat ICD-code-based diagnostic identification as a useful but genuinely imperfect proxy requiring validation, ideally against actual clinical chart review for at least a representative sample, rather than as an automatically reliable ground-truth label.

### 1.7 🟡 What is the concept of data provenance specifically in a multi-source biomedical data integration context, and why does combining EHR data from multiple different health systems introduce specific challenges beyond the single-system data quality concerns already discussed?

Data provenance refers to the documented history and origin of a specific piece of data — which system it came from, what transformations it's undergone, and through what process. Combining EHR data from multiple different health systems introduces specific challenges beyond single-system data quality concerns because different health systems commonly use different EHR software platforms with genuinely different underlying data models and coding conventions, different local clinical documentation and workflow practices, and potentially different patient populations with different baseline characteristics — meaning a variable that appears to represent "the same thing" across two different health systems' data (a specific lab value, or a specific diagnosis category) may not actually be directly comparable without careful harmonization, and a biomedical data scientist working with multi-source data needs to actively verify and document this kind of cross-system comparability rather than assuming that superficially similarly-named or similarly-coded data elements are genuinely equivalent across different source systems, echoing the data harmonization concerns discussed in adjacent longevity therapeutics and precision medicine literature regarding combining data across multiple studies or cohorts.

### 1.8 🟢 What is a common data model (such as OMOP), and why has standardizing multi-institutional biomedical data into a shared common data model become an important practice for large-scale collaborative research?

A common data model is a standardized data structure and vocabulary that different institutions can map their own, often quite differently structured native data into, enabling data from multiple different sources to be analyzed using shared, consistent analytical tools and definitions without requiring each institution's data to be manually and separately reconciled for every new research question, echoing the common data model discussion found in adjacent precision medicine literature. This has become an important practice for large-scale collaborative biomedical research specifically because many clinically important research questions require aggregating data across many institutions to achieve adequate statistical power or population diversity, and a common data model substantially reduces the practical technical and methodological burden of conducting this kind of multi-institutional research — a biomedical data scientist working with multi-institutional data resources should understand the specific common data model being used (its structure, its standard vocabularies, and its known limitations and conventions) as directly relevant to correctly interpreting and analyzing the resulting harmonized dataset, rather than treating the underlying standardization process as an invisible, unproblematic technical detail.

### 1.9 🟡 What is the difference between claims data and EHR data as two genuinely distinct sources of real-world clinical information, and what specific complementary strengths and weaknesses does each carry?

Claims data (insurance billing records) captures healthcare utilization and reimbursement information — what services were billed for, by whom, and when — generally offering broad, longitudinal coverage across a patient's care spanning multiple different providers and health systems (since claims are generated whenever a billable service occurs, regardless of which specific system delivered it), but offering relatively limited clinical detail and depth, since claims data is fundamentally structured around billing categories rather than rich clinical documentation, and generally lacks lab values, detailed clinical notes, or other granular clinical information. EHR data offers considerably richer clinical detail (lab results, vital signs, clinical notes, and more) but is typically limited to whatever care was actually delivered and documented within that specific health system, meaning a patient's care received elsewhere (at a different hospital, or under a different insurance plan) generally won't appear in a single health system's EHR data — a biomedical data scientist should recognize these as genuinely complementary rather than redundant data sources, each offering real value the other typically lacks, and increasingly, linked claims-EHR datasets (combining both sources for the same patients where feasible) are used specifically to leverage both data types' complementary strengths simultaneously.

### 1.10 🟢 What is the difference between prevalence and incidence as basic epidemiological measures, and why is confusing these two measures a common and consequential error in biomedical data analysis?

Prevalence is the proportion of a population that has a given condition at a specific point in time (or over a specified period), while incidence is the rate at which new cases of a condition occur within a population over a specified time period, among individuals who didn't already have the condition. Confusing these two measures is a common and consequential error because they answer genuinely different questions with different implications — prevalence reflects both how frequently a condition newly occurs (incidence) and how long affected individuals remain in that state (duration, which for a chronic condition managed over many years, or a condition affecting mortality, can substantially diverge from incidence) — a biomedical data scientist analyzing disease burden or the effect of an intervention needs to be precise about which of these two measures is actually being calculated and reported, since a change in observed prevalence over time could reflect a genuine change in new-case incidence, a change in survival or disease duration, a change in detection/diagnosis practices, or some combination of all three, and conflating these distinct underlying drivers can lead to a fundamentally mistaken interpretation of what's actually changing and why.

### 1.11 🟡 What is the concept of a patient's "index date" in a clinical research or observational study context, and why does correctly and consistently defining this reference point matter for avoiding several specific, well-documented sources of analytical bias?

An index date is the specific reference date used to define a patient's entry into a cohort or study analysis — typically the date of a specific diagnosis, the date of first exposure to a treatment being studied, or another clinically meaningful anchor point relative to which all other data (prior history, subsequent outcomes) is organized and measured. Correctly and consistently defining this reference point matters for avoiding several specific, well-documented sources of bias — inconsistent or poorly-defined index dates can introduce immortal time bias (discussed in more depth in adjacent precision medicine literature, where a period during which the outcome of interest couldn't have occurred by definition is inadvertently and inappropriately included as "at-risk" follow-up time) if the index date is defined based on something that can only be observed after a patient has already survived or remained event-free for some period, and inconsistent index date definitions across a comparison group can introduce genuine, hard-to-detect confounding if one group's index dates are systematically defined differently (earlier or later relative to the actual clinical event of interest) than the comparison group's — a biomedical data scientist designing an observational study needs to define index dates carefully, consistently, and in a way that avoids these specific, well-documented pitfalls, rather than treating index date selection as an incidental technical detail.

### 1.12 🟢 What is the concept of a look-back period (or baseline period) in an observational study using longitudinal clinical or claims data, and what specific tradeoff does choosing a longer versus shorter look-back window involve?

A look-back or baseline period is a defined window of time before a patient's index date (discussed in category 1.11) used to characterize their pre-existing clinical history, comorbidities, and other baseline characteristics relevant to the study — for instance, a study might require patients to have at least 12 months of continuous data available before their index date, specifically to allow reliable characterization of their baseline health status. Choosing a longer look-back window generally allows for more complete and reliable characterization of a patient's baseline clinical history (capturing conditions or events that might not have been recently active or documented, but that remain clinically relevant), but comes at a specific tradeoff cost — requiring a longer continuous look-back period before study entry tends to reduce the eligible study population (since fewer patients will have this much continuous data available, particularly newer patients to a given health system or a health plan) and can introduce its own selection bias (systematically favoring inclusion of patients with longer-established, more stable care relationships with a given health system, who may differ in clinically relevant ways from patients with shorter documented histories) — a biomedical data scientist needs to weigh this genuine tradeoff explicitly and choose a look-back window duration appropriate to the specific clinical question and condition being studied, rather than defaulting to an arbitrary, unjustified window length.

### 1.13 🟡 What is the difference between a clinical outcome that's directly observed versus one that must be inferred or derived from proxy data elements, and why does a biomedical data scientist need to be especially careful when validating a derived outcome definition?

A directly observed outcome is captured explicitly and unambiguously in the available data (a lab test result crossing a defined clinical threshold, a documented procedure occurring on a specific date). A derived or inferred outcome must instead be constructed from proxy data elements that don't directly and unambiguously represent the outcome of interest — for example, inferring that a patient experienced a specific adverse clinical event based on a combination of diagnosis codes, procedure codes, and medication changes that collectively suggest, but don't directly confirm, that event's occurrence. A biomedical data scientist needs to be especially careful validating this kind of derived outcome definition because the proxy data elements used to construct it may have their own specific sensitivity and specificity limitations (echoing the computable phenotype validation discussion in category 1.3), and an outcome definition that seems clinically reasonable in principle can still perform poorly in practice if the specific proxy elements chosen don't reliably and specifically capture the intended clinical event — rigorous validation (ideally against a manually reviewed reference sample, or against an independent, more directly observed data source when available) is essential before relying on a derived outcome definition for any consequential analysis, rather than assuming a clinically sensible-sounding derivation logic automatically translates into an accurate outcome measure in practice.

### 1.14 🟢 What is the concept of a data dictionary (or data element specification), and why is maintaining one considered essential practice for any biomedical dataset intended for use by multiple analysts or over an extended period of time?

A data dictionary is formal, structured documentation describing exactly what each variable or data element in a dataset represents, its expected format and valid range of values, how it was derived or collected, and any known limitations or caveats relevant to its interpretation. Maintaining a data dictionary is considered essential practice for any biomedical dataset intended for use by multiple analysts or over an extended period specifically because biomedical datasets, particularly those derived from complex sources like EHR or claims data, frequently contain variables whose exact meaning and derivation logic isn't obvious or self-evident from the variable name alone — without clear, accessible documentation, different analysts working with the same underlying dataset risk making genuinely different, potentially inconsistent assumptions about what a given variable actually represents, and even the original analyst who created a derived variable may struggle to accurately recall its exact derivation logic and caveats months or years later without this kind of documentation — a rigorous biomedical data scientist should treat maintaining thorough, accurate, and current data dictionary documentation as a core, non-optional professional responsibility rather than an optional nicety to be addressed only if time permits.

### 1.15 🟡 What is the difference between a patient-level and an encounter-level (or visit-level) unit of analysis in clinical data, and why does choosing the wrong unit of analysis for a given research question risk producing statistically invalid or misleading results?

A patient-level analysis treats each individual patient as a single, independent unit of observation, typically aggregating or summarizing all of that patient's data across however many clinical encounters they had into a single set of patient-level values. An encounter-level analysis instead treats each individual clinical visit or encounter as a separate unit of observation, meaning a single patient with multiple encounters contributes multiple separate observations to the analysis. Choosing the wrong unit of analysis for a given research question risks producing statistically invalid or misleading results specifically because encounters from the same patient aren't genuinely independent observations of each other (a form of the same pseudoreplication concern discussed in adjacent bioinformatics statistics literature regarding confusing technical and biological replicates) — treating multiple encounters from the same patient as though they were independent data points, without using appropriate statistical methods specifically designed to account for this within-patient correlation (such as mixed-effects or hierarchical models, or appropriate clustering-adjusted standard errors), can artificially inflate the apparent statistical sample size and correspondingly produce misleadingly overconfident, artificially narrow statistical results — a biomedical data scientist needs to explicitly identify which unit of analysis is actually appropriate for a given research question and apply statistical methods correctly accounting for this structure, rather than defaulting to a naive analysis that implicitly and incorrectly treats non-independent observations as independent.

### 1.16 🟢 What is the concept of "real-world data" (RWD) and "real-world evidence" (RWE), and how do these terms specifically differ from each other, given that they're sometimes used loosely or interchangeably?

Real-world data refers to data relating to patient health status and healthcare delivery collected from sources outside a traditional controlled clinical trial setting — EHR data, claims data, patient registries, and similar sources reflecting actual, routine clinical practice, echoing the real-world evidence discussion found in adjacent precision medicine literature. Real-world evidence refers specifically to the clinical evidence derived from analyzing this real-world data — meaning RWD is the underlying raw material, while RWE is the analytical output or conclusion drawn from appropriately analyzing that data to answer a specific clinical or research question. This distinction matters because simply having access to real-world data doesn't automatically constitute real-world evidence — generating genuinely valid, reliable RWE from RWD requires the same kind of careful, rigorous analytical methodology (appropriate study design, bias mitigation, statistical rigor) discussed throughout this entire repo, and a biomedical data scientist should be precise about this distinction rather than treating "we have real-world data" as equivalent to "we have generated valid real-world evidence," since the analytical rigor applied to the data is what actually determines whether meaningful, trustworthy evidence results.

### 1.17 🟢 What is the concept of a patient registry, and how does its typical data collection process and purpose differ from both a clinical trial and routine EHR-derived real-world data?

A patient registry is an organized system that collects standardized clinical and outcome data on a specific defined population (typically patients with a particular condition, or patients who received a particular treatment or procedure), generally following a defined, purpose-built data collection protocol similar in spirit to a clinical trial's structured data collection, but without a trial's controlled intervention or randomization — patients in a registry receive whatever care their treating clinicians determine is appropriate, with the registry simply and systematically observing and recording outcomes over time, rather than assigning or controlling treatment. This differs from a clinical trial in lacking randomization and controlled intervention, and differs from routine EHR-derived real-world data in generally offering more standardized, purpose-built, and complete data collection specifically designed around the registry's research or clinical surveillance purpose (often including data elements and follow-up structure not routinely captured in standard clinical care documentation) — a biomedical data scientist should understand registries as occupying a genuinely distinct middle ground between the high internal validity but artificial setting of a randomized trial and the broad but often messier, less purpose-built real-world data available from routine clinical care documentation.

### 1.18 🔴 A health system wants to build a predictive model using five years of historical EHR data, but the system's EHR platform was migrated to a new vendor three years ago. What specific data quality and analytical concerns would you want to investigate before treating this combined dataset as a single, homogeneous data source?

I'd want to work through several specific, systematic concerns rather than assuming the migration was a purely technical event with no meaningful downstream analytical consequence. First, I'd want to understand exactly how the historical data from the prior EHR platform was actually migrated into the new system — whether it was a genuinely complete, faithful migration preserving the original data's full structure and detail, or whether some data elements were lost, simplified, or required lossy reformatting during the transition, which would create a systematic data completeness or quality difference between the pre- and post-migration periods that could easily be mistaken for a genuine clinical trend if not specifically identified and accounted for. Second, I'd want to check whether the specific coding conventions, terminology mappings, or default values used for key variables changed between the two platforms — different EHR vendors sometimes use different underlying data models or default documentation workflows, meaning a variable that appears superficially consistent across the full five-year window (same variable name, same apparent structure) could actually reflect a meaningfully different underlying capture process or definition before versus after the migration, echoing the multi-institutional data harmonization concerns discussed in category 1.7 applied here to a single institution's own before/after migration boundary. Third, I'd want to specifically and directly test for a structural break at the migration date — examining whether key variables' completeness rates, value distributions, or documentation patterns show a clear, discontinuous shift right around the migration date, which would be strong evidence of a technical artifact requiring explicit correction or accounting for, distinct from any genuine underlying clinical trend the model might otherwise be trying to learn from smoothly across this discontinuity. Fourth, given any confirmed differences, I'd want to consider whether the migration date should be explicitly included as a covariate or stratification variable in any resulting predictive model, or whether validating the model's performance separately within the pre- and post-migration periods is warranted, to confirm the model isn't simply learning to detect which EHR platform generated a given record rather than learning genuine, clinically meaningful patterns — this kind of investigation is exactly the disciplined, specific due diligence that should precede treating a multi-year EHR dataset spanning a platform migration as a single, uniformly reliable data source, rather than assuming data quality and structure remained genuinely constant across the entire period simply because it originates from "the same health system."
